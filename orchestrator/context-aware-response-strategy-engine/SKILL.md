# Context-Aware Response Strategy Engine

## Purpose

Select the most appropriate response strategy for a real situation by combining context understanding, relevant psychology, communication design, and domain-specific knowledge. The engine works across business, customer service, leadership, workplace hierarchy, negotiation, professional relationships, family, friendship, and personal relationships.

Its central rule is: **read the situation before choosing the response**. Do not default to persuasion, marketing, apology, compensation, agreement, or emotional reassurance. Choose only what the actual situation calls for.

This is a routing and decision specification. It coordinates specialist skills; it is not a standalone autonomous agent, a clinical assessment, or a guarantee of another person's reaction.

## Core responsibilities

1. Reconstruct the situation from current and relevant prior context.
2. Identify the people, roles, relationship, power differences, audience, channel, and desired outcome.
3. Separate observable facts, reported claims, interpretations, hypotheses, and unknowns.
4. Identify the actual issue, unmet need, expectation gap, objection, risk, or opportunity.
5. Select the smallest useful combination of psychology, communication, marketing, business, or other specialist skills.
6. Decide what kind of response is appropriate before writing it.
7. Produce a response whose wording visibly reflects the selected strategy.
8. Review outcomes and adapt when new evidence appears.

## Operating principles

- **Context before technique:** do not select a tactic from keywords or star ratings alone.
- **Role flexibility:** the user may be a customer, seller, owner, employee, manager, subordinate, peer, partner, friend, or observer.
- **Need before offer:** understand what the other person needs before proposing a solution, benefit, concession, or product.
- **Proportionality:** match the response to the actual issue, impact, evidence, and authority.
- **Selective routing:** use only skills that add a distinct contribution. Do not load every Psychology, Communication, or Marketing skill for every request.
- **Visible outcome:** when drafting, the final message must contain the chosen practical response, not merely a generic polite sentence after hidden analysis.
- **Natural delivery:** internal analysis should usually remain internal. Show it when the user asks for analysis, learning, comparison, or rationale.
- **Evidence discipline:** do not claim certainty about private motives, emotions, or future reactions from sparse signals.
- **Agency:** support informed decisions and clear boundaries; do not assume the goal is to force agreement or control another person.

## Situation reading

Before selecting a strategy, identify only the dimensions relevant to the task.

### 1. People and roles

Identify who is speaking, who will receive the response, their roles, responsibilities, relationship history, and any relevant authority or dependency. Do not assume that one party is automatically right because of their title, customer status, seniority, or rating.

### 2. Goal and desired outcome

Classify the user's immediate goal, such as:
- Understand a person or situation.
- Acknowledge or appreciate someone.
- Explain, clarify, or correct information.
- Resolve a complaint or restore a loss.
- Build trust or maintain a relationship.
- Present value or help someone evaluate an offer.
- Persuade, negotiate, or reach an agreement.
- Give feedback, set expectations, or enforce a boundary.
- De-escalate conflict or protect safety.
- Draft a public response for both the direct recipient and wider audience.

Distinguish the user's desired outcome from what the other person is free to decide.

### 3. Facts, evidence, and uncertainty

Record relevant words, actions, order details, promises, terms, dates, prior decisions, and outcomes. Separate confirmed information from the user's account, interpretations, and hypotheses. Identify missing facts only when they could change the decision. Ask one focused question when necessary; otherwise proceed with explicit uncertainty.

### 4. Need, objection, and expectation gap

Determine what is actually at issue. Examples include:
- A practical loss or unfulfilled promise.
- A mismatch between expected and received value.
- A preference or subjective opinion.
- A lack of information or misunderstanding.
- A need for recognition, clarity, support, autonomy, or fairness.
- A disagreement over priorities, terms, responsibility, or boundaries.
- A concern about risk, credibility, price, quality, or effort.

Do not turn every negative reaction into a business failure or every disagreement into a psychological problem.

### 5. Audience and channel

Decide whether the message is private or public, one-to-one or group-facing, spoken or written, formal or casual. For public replies, consider both the person being addressed and reasonable observers. Do not expose private information to serve a public messaging goal.

## Cross-case and multimodal evidence handling

When the user supplies several reviews, messages, order records, or product images, do not treat each item as an isolated writing task if the request is to understand the overall situation.

### Batch review and pattern analysis

1. Separate each customer's individual experience from patterns across customers.
2. Group reports by concrete issue (for example: portion, missing item, taste, temperature, delay, staff interaction, or unclear menu information).
3. Count occurrences only when the supplied material supports a count. State the sample size and time period when known; do not present a small or selected sample as representative of all customers.
4. Distinguish a repeated symptom from a confirmed root cause. Several portion complaints may indicate a portion-control issue, but the cause still needs checking against recipe standards, serving tools, staff practice, and order records.
5. Prioritize patterns by severity, potential harm, frequency, customer impact, and operational feasibility. A single credible safety report can require action even when it is not frequent.
6. Produce two separate outputs when useful: (a) the appropriate response to each customer and (b) the operational issue that the business should investigate. Do not replace an individual response with a generic batch reply.
7. State confidence and missing evidence. Do not invent customer history, motives, or a trend from unrelated examples.

### Visual and other non-text evidence

When images, screenshots, menu photos, receipts, or product comparisons are supplied, inspect only what is actually visible and relevant. Compare the visible product with the stated menu description, agreed offer, or documented standard. Separate direct observations from estimates and unknowns; camera angle, lighting, perspective, packaging, and image cropping can distort apparent size, color, and condition. Do not claim an image proves taste, freshness, weight, temperature, or the exact order received unless the evidence supports it. If the comparison could change a refund, safety, or other consequential decision, request the missing order or measurement detail rather than guessing.

## Response strategy selection

Select a primary strategy and add a supporting strategy only when useful.

| Situation | Primary response strategy | Possible supporting strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Person is satisfied or offers praise | Appreciation and reinforcement | Genuine product or relationship strength; low-pressure continuation |
| Person is dissatisfied but no material failure is established | Acknowledge and clarify | Explain real value; record feedback; correct expectations |
| Business or service provider caused a verifiable loss | Service recovery and restoration | Apology, proportionate remedy, prevention, trust signal |
| Person questions price or value | Value explanation and objection handling | Relevant comparison, scope clarification, proof |
| Potential buyer is evaluating an offer | Needs-based marketing and persuasion | Benefit framing, evidence, risk clarification, suitable call to action |
| Person is confused or missing information | Clarification and explanation | Active listening and concise education |
| Manager gives feedback to an employee | Clear accountability and developmental feedback | Expectations, evidence, support, measurable next step |
| Employee raises a concern to a manager | Assertive upward communication | Evidence, impact, options, requested decision |
| Peers disagree | Collaborative problem solving | Perspective-taking, role clarity, negotiation |
| Negotiation is underway | Interest-based negotiation | Alternatives, trade-offs, boundaries, written confirmation |
| Personal relationship needs support | Empathy and connection | Listening, needs clarification, boundaries |
| Someone crosses a legitimate boundary | Respectful firmness and boundary setting | Consequences, documentation, escalation where appropriate |
| Safety or serious harm may be involved | Risk containment and responsible escalation | Clear communication and qualified support |

The table is a starting map, not a rigid script. Use the actual context to choose or combine strategies.

## Compensation and concession decision

A complaint, low rating, objection, or negative emotion does not automatically justify a free item, discount, refund, apology, or concession.

Use this sequence:

1. **Identify the promise or obligation.** What was ordered, agreed, described, or reasonably expected?
2. **Check what happened.** What was actually delivered or done? What evidence is available?
3. **Assess impact.** Was there a material loss, inconvenience, safety concern, or merely a preference difference?
4. **Correct the failure first.** Restore the missing value or fulfill the original commitment where appropriate.
5. **Consider additional goodwill separately.** Offer an extra benefit only when the impact, relationship context, policy, economics, and approval justify it.
6. **Check authority and feasibility.** Do not promise a refund, free product, discount, schedule change, or other concession without authorization and ability to fulfill it.
7. **Do not purchase approval.** A remedy must not depend on a positive review, rating change, silence, endorsement, or deletion of criticism.

Track the commitment state explicitly: **considered** (internal option), **recommended** (proposed to the owner), **approved** (authorized and feasible), **offered** (communicated to the customer), and **fulfilled** (actually delivered). Never describe a proposed or approved remedy as already offered or completed. If the user has not authorized a concession and policy does not clearly authorize it, present it as a recommendation for approval rather than customer-facing promise.

Distinguish an existing inclusion from an additional benefit. Never market something already included as though it were a new gift. For safety-related complaints, prioritize safety and investigation rather than using a promotional gesture.

## Marketing decision and visibility

Marketing is relevant when the context involves a buyer, customer, market audience, offer, product, service, brand, or public business reputation. It is not automatically relevant to every interpersonal message.

When marketing is relevant:

1. Identify the audience's actual need or objection.
2. Select one truthful value proposition or trust signal that addresses it.
3. Use evidence, real product attributes, clear scope, or an approved offer.
4. Make the key benefit easy to notice in the final message.
5. Use a clear next step only when it fits the relationship and situation.

For a public customer response, write for both:
- **The reviewer:** address their specific experience and fair next step.
- **The silent reader:** make a relevant benefit, accountability action, or trust signal understandable at a glance.

For a positive review, reinforce what the customer genuinely praised; do not invent an extra gift. For a neutral or subjective concern, acknowledge and clarify without automatically compensating. For a verified service failure, state the approved remedy clearly. Do not turn a complaint into a sales pitch before addressing the problem.

### Required marketing opportunity assessment for business reviews

When the context involves a business, product, service, buyer, customer review, or public brand response, marketing opportunity detection is a required assessment step—not an automatic requirement to advertise.

Before drafting, explicitly evaluate:
1. **Product and offer:** Which real product, menu item, package, service feature, or existing inclusion is relevant?
2. **Differentiation:** What verified product strength, distinctive attribute, quality, convenience, or experience can give a buyer a reason to choose the business?
3. **Value and objection:** What value proposition can address the reviewer's or silent reader's likely price, portion, quality, trust, or convenience concern?
4. **Commercial opportunity:** Is there a relevant existing bundle, cross-sell, add-on, bonus, or approved offer that fits this situation?
5. **Conversion path:** Would a natural invitation or next step help, or would it feel inappropriate at this moment?

Evaluate these opportunities even when the final decision is not to use them. If product/menu information is unavailable, do not invent a USP, benefit, bonus, or offer; use only verified facts and identify the missing information when it materially limits the recommendation.

The final public reply should combine two distinct jobs where appropriate: resolve or acknowledge the reviewer's experience, and make one relevant product value, differentiator, approved benefit, or credible trust signal visible to prospective buyers. Do not let the service-recovery task crowd out the marketing assessment, and do not let marketing replace a fair remedy.

Record the marketing decision internally as one of:
- **Use:** a relevant, factual value proposition or offer belongs in the reply.
- **Defer:** address the complaint first; a later invitation may be appropriate after resolution.
- **Omit:** no relevant or sufficiently verified marketing message fits this case.

A decision to defer or omit must be based on the customer's condition, relevance, evidence, timing, and commercial fit—not simply because the review is negative.

## Psychology integration

Use psychological concepts as explanatory lenses, not as certainty about hidden mental states.

Potentially relevant lenses include:
- Procedural, distributive, and interactional fairness.
- Perceived value, reference points, anchoring, and loss aversion.
- Social proof, trust signals, reciprocity, and commitment.
- Motivation, autonomy, competence, belonging, and recognition.
- Emotional regulation, cognitive load, uncertainty, and face-saving.
- Power, dependency, status, incentives, and ability to decline.
- Active listening, perspective-taking, and expectation management.

Select only concepts that change the recommended action or wording. Do not add psychological jargon to a message intended for ordinary readers.

## Response construction

Before drafting, decide:
- What should the recipient understand or feel acknowledged about?
- What is the one most important point?
- What action, explanation, offer, boundary, or question is appropriate?
- What tone and level of directness fit the relationship?
- What information must remain private?
- What should the recipient do next, if anything?

Then write in the user's requested language and voice. Keep simple responses concise. For public-facing marketing or reputation replies, make the relevant benefit, remedy, or trust signal scannable. Avoid generic filler, defensive counterarguments, false urgency, invented benefits, unsupported claims, and unnecessary calls to action.

## Cross-domain routing

- **Contextual Understanding & Intent Inference:** reconstructs relevant prior context and preserves decisions and constraints.
- **Situational Intelligence & Contextual Reasoning:** compares interacting facts, options, feasibility, and consequences.
- **Human Behavior Intelligence Layer:** coordinates complex human, relational, social, and power dynamics.
- **Psychology Decision Engine:** selects focused behavioral and psychological knowledge.
- **Communication Decision Engine:** designs listening, wording, negotiation, feedback, and delivery.
- **Service Recovery Marketing & Trust Building:** handles customer complaints, remedies, public reviews, and recovery follow-through.
- **Consumer Psychology & Persuasion:** handles buyer behavior, value perception, offers, and persuasion.
- **Brand Communication & Public Relations:** handles public statements and reputation messaging.
- **Business Operations & Scaling:** addresses operational causes and repeat failures.
- **Risk Assessment & Consequence Intelligence:** evaluates material safety, legal, financial, exploitation, or other high-impact consequences.

The Context-Aware Response Strategy Engine chooses the lead specialist based on the actual task. It must not duplicate specialist content or route every request through every layer.

## User-facing output modes

- **Practical mode (default):** provide the recommended action or ready-to-use response. Keep internal routing and analysis hidden unless useful.
- **Analysis mode:** explain the situation reading, known facts, uncertainties, selected strategy, and why alternatives were rejected.
- **Learning mode:** explain the relevant psychological, communication, or marketing mechanisms and show how to apply them in other contexts.
- **Comparison mode:** compare plausible response strategies and their likely trade-offs without claiming certainty about outcomes.

If the user explicitly asks for a draft, provide the complete usable draft. If the user asks why a strategy was selected, expose the reasoning at an appropriate level. Do not force a report format onto a simple request.

## Quality check

Before responding, verify:
- Did I read the actual situation rather than react to one keyword or rating?
- Did I identify the user's role, recipient, relationship, and goal?
- Did I separate facts from assumptions?
- Did I identify the actual problem or need?
- Did I choose the correct primary response strategy?
- Did I avoid unnecessary compensation, persuasion, or marketing?
- If marketing is relevant, is its value or trust signal visible in the final wording?
- For a business review, did I actively assess product differentiation, value proposition, real inclusions, approved offers, and a suitable conversion path—even if I decide to defer or omit promotion?
- Does the final reply give prospective buyers a truthful reason to consider the product without using the reviewer as an advertising prop?
- If a remedy is offered, is it proportionate, approved, and feasible?
- Does the response fit the audience, channel, and user's requested tone?
- Have I preserved privacy, agency, and relevant boundaries?

## Evidence and limitations

This is an original AI operational framework. It synthesizes and coordinates existing repository knowledge; it is not a direct adaptation of a single book, a validated psychological test, or a guarantee of customer conversion or relationship outcomes. Specialist skills remain the source of detailed domain methods. Runtime retrieval, persistent context, and autonomous execution require separate implementation.

## Success criteria

The engine succeeds when the chosen response fits the real situation, addresses the relevant need, uses specialist knowledge without unnecessary duplication, communicates the selected strategy clearly, avoids disproportionate or unauthorized commitments, and adapts when new evidence appears.

## Skill boundary governance

Follow [Skill Boundary & Routing Governance](skill-boundary-governance/SKILL.md). This engine owns the response-mode decision only. Accept the context packet and situation model from their respective owners; do not rebuild them unless new evidence exposes a material gap. Select acknowledge, clarify, inform, apologize, remedy, negotiate, persuade, set a boundary, decline, or no further action based on the case. Hand the selected mode and rationale to Communication for wording. Marketing and compensation are optional outcomes, never defaults.
