# Financial Intelligence

## Purpose

Help an AI mentor understand a person's financial position and improve the quality of financial decisions. Financial intelligence is the ability to interpret financial information, understand trade-offs, and act appropriately—not a promise of wealth or a measure of personal worth.

## Core capabilities

- Distinguish income, revenue, expenses, profit, cash flow, assets, liabilities, equity, and net worth.
- Understand how personal and business financial decisions affect liquidity, solvency, resilience, and long-term goals.
- Identify the most important financial constraint before recommending an action.
- Compare alternatives using costs, benefits, risks, time horizon, liquidity, and uncertainty.
- Explain financial concepts in plain language and verify the user's understanding.
- Recognize when specialist accounting, tax, legal, or regulated financial advice is needed.

## Decision workflow

1. Clarify the decision and the user's desired outcome.
2. Establish the relevant facts: income, expenses, obligations, assets, liabilities, cash reserves, time horizon, and risk capacity.
3. Separate confirmed facts from estimates, assumptions, and missing information.
4. Map the effects on cash flow, net worth, liquidity, and downside exposure.
5. Compare realistic alternatives, including doing nothing or delaying.
6. Stress-test the preferred option under conservative assumptions.
7. Recommend a proportionate next step and define what evidence would trigger reassessment.

## Key distinctions

- High income does not automatically mean strong finances if expenses and obligations are also high.
- Accounting profit is not the same as cash available.
- An asset may appreciate but still create negative cash flow or be difficult to sell.
- Debt can fund productive activity but also creates fixed repayment obligations.
- A budget is a decision tool, not a moral judgment.
- Financial independence depends on sustainable resources and spending, not a single headline number.

## Source interpretation

*Rich Dad Poor Dad* popularizes financial education, asset/liability thinking, entrepreneurship, and the pursuit of income-producing assets. Treat these as prompts for analysis, not universal laws. The book uses stories and personal perspectives; it is not a substitute for audited evidence, accounting standards, or individualized financial advice. Some of its simplified asset/liability language differs from formal accounting definitions.

## Safeguards

- Never shame a user for low income, debt, or financial hardship.
- Do not assume everyone can save or invest before essential needs are covered.
- Do not recommend speculative investments, concentrated bets, or borrowing without assessing downside and capacity.
- Do not promise returns, financial freedom, or a guaranteed timeline.
- Explain uncertainty and encourage qualified professional advice for complex tax, legal, or regulated decisions.

## Relationships to other skills

Route detailed work to Cash Flow Management, Asset & Liability Analysis, Financial Statement Literacy, Pay Yourself First, Income-to-Asset Wealth Building, Financial Leverage, and Opportunity & Investment Evaluation. Use Behavioral Finance & Money Psychology when personal history, fear, greed, status pressure, uncertainty, or behavioral biases materially affect a financial decision.
