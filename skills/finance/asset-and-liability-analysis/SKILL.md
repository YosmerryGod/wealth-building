# Asset & Liability Analysis

## Purpose

Help users classify and evaluate what they own and owe, with special attention to cash-flow effects, liquidity, risk, and economic purpose.

## Two lenses that must not be confused

1. **Accounting lens:** Assets are resources controlled by an entity as a result of past events; liabilities are present obligations. Classification follows applicable accounting standards and context.
2. **Personal cash-flow lens:** For household decision-making, examine whether an item tends to bring money in, consume money, preserve value, or create future obligations.

The popular *Rich Dad Poor Dad* shorthand—assets put money in your pocket and liabilities take money out—is a useful cash-flow prompt, but it is not a complete accounting definition. A home, vehicle, or investment may have different classifications and cash-flow effects depending on ownership, financing, use, and circumstances.

## Evaluation process

For each material item, record:
- Ownership and legal control.
- Purchase price and current estimated value, with valuation date and source.
- Outstanding debt and repayment terms.
- Income generated, if any.
- Recurring costs: maintenance, insurance, tax, fees, utilities, and management.
- Liquidity and realistic time/cost to sell.
- Volatility, concentration, and downside exposure.
- Strategic or personal utility that may not be captured by cash flow.

## Practical categories

- Cash and cash equivalents: liquid resources, subject to institution and currency risk.
- Income-producing assets: businesses, rental property, equipment, or securities that may generate income but carry costs and risk.
- Use assets: housing, vehicles, tools, or equipment primarily used for living or work.
- Financial investments: deposits, bonds, shares, funds, and other instruments with varying risks and liquidity.
- Liabilities: mortgages, consumer loans, business loans, credit-card balances, taxes payable, and other obligations.

Do not automatically call a use asset a bad purchase. Housing, transport, education, and tools can create meaningful utility or earning capacity.

## Decision workflow

1. Identify the user's question: balance-sheet classification, purchase decision, portfolio review, or cash-flow improvement.
2. Gather reliable values and dates; label estimates.
3. Separate gross asset value from equity after debt.
4. Calculate recurring net cash contribution where relevant.
5. Assess liquidity, volatility, concentration, and downside.
6. Compare the item with alternatives, including renting, leasing, delaying, or not purchasing.
7. Explain the result in both accounting and practical cash-flow terms.

## Useful calculations

- Net worth = total assets − total liabilities.
- Asset equity = estimated asset value − debt secured against it.
- Net cash contribution = cash income − recurring cash costs attributable to the asset.
- Cash-on-cash return = annual pre-tax cash flow ÷ cash invested, when the numerator and denominator are defined consistently.

State exclusions and assumptions. Do not treat appreciation as guaranteed income or use gross rent/revenue as net return.

## Safeguards

- Avoid simplistic claims that every home or vehicle is a liability or every investment is an asset in the same practical sense.
- Include transaction costs, taxes, maintenance, vacancy, depreciation, and financing where relevant.
- Do not infer current market value without evidence.
- Do not recommend selling essential assets without assessing consequences.
- Do not ignore legal ownership, joint ownership, guarantees, or contingent liabilities.
