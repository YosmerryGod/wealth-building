# Business Operations & Scaling

## Purpose

Help an AI mentor turn a validated business idea into a functioning operation and grow it in stages. This skill covers the practical system between the first sale and a larger, more repeatable business: production, capacity, people, inventory, quality, sales channels, operating cash, and expansion decisions.

This skill complements Business Opportunity Discovery, which focuses on finding and validating opportunities, and Financial Leverage, which focuses on reinvestment and responsible borrowing. It does not replace accounting, legal, tax, food-safety, agricultural, or other industry-specific expertise.

## Source example: oyster mushroom cultivation

The user-provided video presents an oyster mushroom business as an example of starting with very limited capital and gradually increasing production capacity. It describes a progression from a small trial to a larger operation, selling fresh mushrooms through several channels, and using workers as production grows.

The video also presents illustrative figures, including:
- A claimed initial budget of approximately Rp100,000.
- A later production scale described as approximately 2,500 baglogs per month.
- A claimed output of around 33 kg of mushrooms per day.
- A revenue and expense illustration that results in a claimed annual net profit of approximately Rp100.2 million.

These are claims and assumptions shown in the video, not independently verified operating records. The figures do not establish that the initial capital alone funded the later expansion. Before using them in a real plan, verify baglog costs, production cycles, contamination and crop-loss rates, labor, rent, utilities, packaging, transport, selling prices, unsold output, working capital, and actual buyer demand.

The transferable lesson is staged operating growth—not a promise that a small amount of money will automatically become a particular annual profit.

## Operating system

### 1. Translate the business model into daily operations

Define:
- What is produced or delivered.
- The customer and quality standard.
- The production or service process from input to delivery.
- Required equipment, space, suppliers, and skills.
- The time required for each production cycle.
- The capacity limit at each stage.
- The person responsible for each recurring task.

Map the process before increasing volume. Identify bottlenecks, handoffs, failure points, and tasks that depend on one person.

### 2. Plan capacity before promising sales

Calculate capacity using realistic operating assumptions:
- Theoretical capacity: maximum output if everything runs as planned.
- Practical capacity: output after normal downtime, maintenance, labor limits, and process constraints.
- Saleable output: practical output after defects, spoilage, contamination, rework, or quality rejection.

For agricultural or perishable products, account for biological cycles, harvest variability, shelf life, and seasonality. Do not treat a daily average as guaranteed daily output.

Only commit to customer volumes that the operation can reliably fulfill.

### 3. Control inputs and inventory

Track:
- Raw materials and supplier lead times.
- Purchase quantities, unit costs, and minimum order sizes.
- Work in progress and production batches.
- Finished goods, shelf life, and storage conditions.
- Waste, spoilage, defects, and unexplained stock differences.
- Reorder points and safety stock.

Use batch records where traceability matters. For each batch, record input quantities, production date, output, rejects, and direct costs.

Avoid buying large quantities solely to obtain a lower unit price if the stock may expire, degrade, or tie up essential cash.

### 4. Establish quality and process controls

Create simple standard operating procedures (SOPs) for repeatable and safety-critical work:
- Preparation and sanitation.
- Production steps and process parameters.
- Inspection and acceptance criteria.
- Packaging, storage, and delivery.
- Equipment cleaning and maintenance.
- Handling complaints, returns, and failed batches.

Record defects and their causes. Fix recurring causes rather than relying on extra labor or repeated manual correction.

For food, agriculture, and other regulated activities, identify and follow applicable local safety, licensing, and compliance requirements.

### 5. Build sales and distribution around real demand

Identify distinct customer channels and evaluate each separately. Examples may include:
- Direct-to-consumer sales.
- Traditional markets and resellers.
- Retail shops and small merchants.
- Restaurants, food-service buyers, or business customers.
- Processors or other bulk buyers.

For every channel, measure:
- Actual selling price after discounts.
- Order size and frequency.
- Delivery and packaging cost.
- Payment terms and collection time.
- Returns, rejected goods, and unsold stock.
- Repeat-order rate and customer concentration.

Do not count verbal interest, social-media attention, or a potential reseller as revenue. Seek paid orders and repeat purchases. Avoid depending on one buyer unless the risk and fallback plan are understood.

### 6. Manage people and responsibilities

Hire when recurring workload, required skills, service levels, or owner capacity justify the cost—not simply because the business appears to be growing.

Before hiring:
- List the work that needs to be done and its frequency.
- Estimate the hours and skills required.
- Compare employee, contractor, and owner-operated options where lawful.
- Include wages, benefits, training, supervision, and applicable employer obligations.
- Define responsibilities, quality standards, and escalation paths.

Document tasks so the operation does not depend entirely on undocumented knowledge held by one worker.

### 7. Manage operating cash and unit economics

Keep revenue, gross profit, operating profit, and cash flow separate.

For each product or service, calculate:
- Selling price per unit.
- Variable cost per unit.
- Contribution margin per unit.
- Expected saleable volume.
- Fixed operating costs.
- Break-even volume.
- Cash tied up in inventory and receivables.
- Cash required before customer payment arrives.

Include labor, rent, utilities, maintenance, packaging, delivery, platform fees, marketing, spoilage, taxes, financing costs, and the owner's time where relevant.

A business can show accounting profit and still run short of cash if it must pay suppliers and workers before customers pay.

Maintain a working-capital reserve and a clear separation between business and personal funds.

## Scaling framework

### Stage 1: Small controlled test

- Produce or deliver a limited quantity.
- Confirm product quality and process repeatability.
- Find real paying customers.
- Measure actual costs, waste, and time.
- Identify the largest operational constraint.

Do not scale until the business can reliably fulfill a small number of paid orders.

### Stage 2: Repeatable operation

- Document core SOPs.
- Establish supplier and quality controls.
- Track inventory and batch-level costs.
- Build repeat orders across appropriate channels.
- Measure contribution margin and cash conversion.
- Reduce avoidable defects and bottlenecks.

### Stage 3: Capacity expansion

Before adding equipment, space, workers, or production volume:
1. Confirm that demand exceeds current saleable capacity.
2. Identify the actual bottleneck.
3. Estimate the full expansion cost, including working capital.
4. Model conservative, expected, and downside cases.
5. Confirm that supply, labor, quality, storage, and distribution can support the added output.
6. Expand in increments where possible.
7. Compare actual results with the forecast before expanding again.

Do not add capacity based only on optimistic forecasts or a single unusually strong sales period.

### Stage 4: System-led growth

- Delegate documented responsibilities.
- Use simple operating dashboards and regular reviews.
- Monitor quality, delivery, inventory, cash, and customer retention.
- Build backup suppliers and contingency plans.
- Standardize only where it improves reliability.
- Keep management capacity in line with operational complexity.

## Core operating metrics

| Area | Useful metrics |
|---|---|
| Production | Output per day or batch, practical capacity utilization, yield |
| Quality | Defect rate, contamination or spoilage rate, returns |
| Inventory | Stock turnover, days of inventory, waste, stock variance |
| Sales | Paid orders, repeat-order rate, average order size, channel mix |
| Delivery | On-time fulfillment, delivery cost per order |
| Finance | Contribution margin, break-even volume, operating cash flow |
| People | Labor hours per unit, training completion, rework |
| Growth | Incremental capacity, incremental profit, payback period |

Choose a small set of metrics that lead to action. A metric should have a clear definition, data source, review frequency, and owner.

## Expansion decision checklist

Before approving expansion, the AI should be able to answer:
- Is there verified, repeatable demand for the additional output?
- What is the current bottleneck?
- What additional fixed and variable costs will arise?
- How much working capital is needed and when?
- Can quality and delivery remain reliable?
- What happens if sales, output, or prices are lower than expected?
- How long is the payback period under conservative assumptions?
- What measurable result will trigger the next expansion step?
- What condition would cause the business to pause, reduce capacity, or change direction?

If critical information is missing, state the uncertainty and recommend a small test or data-gathering step.

## AI behavior rules

- Inspect the actual business, process, and constraints before recommending expansion.
- Ask focused questions conversationally; do not overwhelm the user with a long questionnaire.
- Distinguish source claims, user-provided facts, verified data, estimates, and assumptions.
- Never present a video's revenue or profit illustration as independently verified.
- Never confuse revenue with profit or cash available for expansion.
- Include the owner's labor and all material operating costs.
- Prefer measured demand and staged investment over speculative capacity.
- Do not recommend borrowing merely to increase output; consult Financial Leverage and assess debt service using conservative cash flow.
- Do not recommend hiring or purchasing equipment without identifying the operational need and full cost.
- Flag industry-specific safety, legal, licensing, and tax requirements for local verification.
- Review actual operating results after each stage and update the plan.
