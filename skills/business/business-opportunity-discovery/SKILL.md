# Business Opportunity Discovery Through Consumer Psychology

## Purpose

Help an AI mentor guide a person who says they are confused about what business to start. The AI should not immediately produce a generic list of trending business ideas. It should first understand the person, discover real customer needs, identify opportunities, evaluate business economics, and recommend low-cost validation before significant investment.

This skill is a decision framework for discovering and testing business opportunities. It does not guarantee that any idea will succeed.

## Source idea: people buy outcomes, not only products

The reviewed video illustrates that businesses can appeal to underlying human needs, desires, emotions, and concerns. Its examples include fragrance, beauty products, companionship or assistance, children's education-related services, and entertainment or experiences.

The useful underlying question is:

**What outcome, need, desire, or problem is the customer paying to address?**

A product's physical features are only one part of its value. Customers may also be buying convenience, confidence, belonging, reassurance, saved time, enjoyment, or reduced uncertainty.

The video includes illustrative prices and business examples. Those figures are not independently verified market data and must not be treated as proof of profitability.

## AI behavior when a user does not know what business to start

### 1. Understand the person before suggesting ideas

Ask conversationally for the information that materially affects feasibility:

- Available starting capital and how much must remain untouched as an emergency reserve.
- Skills, work experience, interests, and existing resources.
- Location, reachable customer groups, and distribution access.
- Time available and whether the user will operate the business personally.
- Income target and expected time horizon.
- Risk tolerance, obligations, and constraints.

Do not ask every question at once if a smaller number of questions can move the conversation forward. Do not assume the user has capital, experience, or time that they have not stated.

### 2. Discover customer needs and buying motivation

Explore the market from the customer's point of view:

- What recurring problem or unmet need exists?
- Who experiences it, and how often?
- What do people currently do to solve it?
- What is frustrating, expensive, slow, inconvenient, or unreliable about current alternatives?
- What outcome would make a customer willing to pay?
- Is the purchase driven mainly by necessity, convenience, aspiration, emotion, risk reduction, or enjoyment?

Use consumer psychology to understand motivation, not to exploit vulnerability. Emotional marketing must remain truthful and must not rely on deception, fabricated scarcity, shame, fearmongering, or unsupported claims.

### 3. Turn needs into business opportunities

Generate a small, relevant shortlist rather than a long generic list. For each opportunity, explain:

- Target customer.
- Customer problem or desired outcome.
- Proposed product or service.
- Why customers might choose it over existing alternatives.
- How the business would reach customers.
- Main operating requirements and constraints.

Prefer opportunities that fit the user's capabilities, resources, reachable market, and risk limits.

### 4. Evaluate the business model

Assess each candidate using evidence where available and label assumptions clearly:

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Demand | Is there evidence that customers want this and will pay? |
| Customer segment | Who exactly is the buyer? |
| Competition | What alternatives already serve the need? |
| Differentiation | Why would customers choose this offer? |
| Startup capital | What must be purchased before the first sale? |
| Unit economics | What are selling price, variable cost, contribution margin, and acquisition cost? |
| Distribution | How will customers discover, order, and receive the offer? |
| Repeat purchase | Is the need recurring, occasional, or one-time? |
| Scalability | Can volume grow without costs or complexity growing faster? |
| Risk | What could make the idea fail, and how costly would failure be? |

Do not confuse revenue, gross margin, operating profit, and cash flow. Include labor, rent, delivery, platform fees, marketing, spoilage, taxes, financing, and the owner's time where relevant.

### 5. Validate before committing significant capital

Recommend the smallest practical experiment that can test the riskiest assumption:

- Interview potential customers about their current behavior, not only whether they like the idea.
- Test a simple offer or prototype.
- Run a small pre-order or paid pilot where appropriate.
- Test a limited geographic area or customer segment.
- Use a small, capped marketing budget.
- Track inquiries, conversion to paid orders, repeat purchases, contribution margin, and customer feedback.

Set a clear test period, budget limit, success threshold, and stop-or-adjust condition. Interest, likes, views, and compliments are not equivalent to paid demand.

### 6. Make a recommendation with uncertainty visible

After comparing options:

1. State the strongest candidate and why it fits the user.
2. Explain the evidence supporting it.
3. Separate verified facts, user-provided facts, estimates, and assumptions.
4. Identify the biggest unanswered risk.
5. Propose a low-cost validation plan.
6. Explain what result would justify investing more and what result would mean changing direction.

If the available information is insufficient, ask the single most important next question instead of pretending to know the answer.

## Consumer psychology supporting concepts

- **Consumer Psychology:** Understand how customers perceive value and make purchase decisions.
- **Pain Point Analysis:** Identify problems, friction, and unmet needs customers want resolved.
- **Emotional Marketing:** Communicate relevant emotional outcomes honestly and respectfully.
- **Value Proposition:** Explain the concrete reason a customer should choose the offer.
- **Persuasive Copywriting:** Present a clear, truthful offer and call to action without manipulation.
- **Market Research:** Check whether the need and willingness to pay exist in the actual target market.

These are supporting capabilities. They do not replace demand validation or financial analysis.

## Example interaction pattern

User: "I have some capital but I don't know what business to start."

Preferred AI approach:

1. Ask about the user's capital range, skills or experience, and location or reachable customers.
2. Identify customer groups and needs that the user can realistically serve.
3. Present a short list of tailored opportunities, not trend-based guesses.
4. Compare startup requirements, unit economics, competition, and risks.
5. Choose one opportunity to test with a small experiment.
6. Review actual results before recommending expansion or borrowing.

## Guardrails

- Never promise fast wealth, guaranteed profit, or certain demand.
- Do not recommend an idea solely because it is viral or popular.
- Do not invent market size, customer demand, prices, or competitor data.
- Distinguish source-derived ideas from the AI's analytical framework and external research.
- Do not encourage the user to risk essential savings or take debt before validating demand and cash flow.
- Respect customers: understand emotional motivations without manipulating fear, insecurity, or dependency.

## Customer segmentation and buying-motivation analysis

Before recommending an opportunity, segment the market by observable differences that affect the purchase decision. Do not treat everyone who might use a product as one buyer group.

For each relevant segment, identify:

- **Buyer and user:** who pays, who uses the product, and whether they are the same person.
- **Need and situation:** the job to be done, pain point, desired outcome, and when the need occurs.
- **Purchase trigger:** the event or condition that makes the customer act now rather than later.
- **Buying behavior:** frequency, preferred channel, decision speed, comparison habits, and who influences or approves the purchase.
- **Budget and price sensitivity:** likely spending capacity, acceptable price range, and what the customer currently spends on alternatives. Treat these as hypotheses until validated.
- **Decision criteria:** quality, convenience, speed, trust, location, status, reliability, service, or other factors that actually matter to this segment.
- **Barriers and risk:** affordability, uncertainty, habit, switching effort, lack of trust, access, or competing priorities.
- **Evidence:** observed behavior, paid orders, interviews, repeat purchases, search or market evidence, and unresolved assumptions.

### Distinguish need, desire, and willingness to pay

Analyze separately:

1. **Need:** a problem or requirement the customer wants resolved.
2. **Desire:** the outcome or experience the customer would prefer.
3. **Buying motive:** the practical or emotional reason behind choosing an offer, such as saving time, convenience, confidence, belonging, enjoyment, recognition, or reducing risk.
4. **Willingness to pay:** whether the customer will exchange money for the proposed solution at a specific price.
5. **Ability to pay:** whether the customer can realistically afford it without unacceptable strain.

A strong desire does not prove willingness or ability to pay. Compliments and stated interest are weaker evidence than deposits, paid trials, completed purchases, and repeat orders.

### Segment prioritization

Compare candidate segments using:

- Severity and frequency of the problem.
- Urgency and strength of the purchase trigger.
- Ability and willingness to pay.
- Reachability and cost of acquiring customers.
- Competition and quality of existing alternatives.
- Repeat-purchase potential and contribution margin.
- Fit with the user's resources and operational capacity.

Recommend a segment only when its attractiveness is supported by evidence or clearly labeled assumptions. Explain why it is a better initial target than the alternatives.

### Required opportunity chain

Make the reasoning traceable:

**Human problem or desire → specific customer segment → purchase situation and motivation → offer → reason to choose → reachable channel → paid validation → unit economics.**

If a link in this chain is unsupported, state what is unknown and design the smallest practical test. Do not jump from an interesting human desire directly to a business recommendation.
