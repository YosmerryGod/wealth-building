# Debt & Installment Management

## Purpose

Help users understand the full cost and cash-flow impact of borrowing, manage existing repayment obligations, compare repayment strategies, and avoid debt structures that are fragile relative to their income.

## Debt inventory

For each obligation, record:
- Creditor and debt type.
- Outstanding principal or settlement balance.
- Interest/profit rate and whether it is fixed or variable.
- Effective annual cost where it can be calculated.
- Fees, insurance, administration, penalties, and late charges.
- Minimum payment and installment amount.
- Due date and remaining term.
- Collateral, guarantees, and consequences of default.
- Early repayment or settlement rules.
- Whether the account is current, overdue, disputed, or in collections.

Distinguish credit-card revolving balances, personal loans, vehicle or home financing, Buy Now Pay Later (BNPL), informal loans, business borrowing, and supplier credit. Their legal treatment and cost structures may differ.

## Understand the true cost

Do not compare products using the advertised monthly installment alone. Include:
- Interest or financing margin.
- Origination and administration fees.
- Insurance and required add-ons.
- Late-payment penalties.
- Balloon or residual payments.
- Early settlement fees.
- Currency or variable-rate exposure.
- Collateral and guarantee risk.

When comparing offers, use total repayment and effective cost over the same period. Explain assumptions where the effective rate cannot be reliably derived.

## Affordability and cash-flow fit

1. Calculate reliable net income and timing.
2. List essential living costs and contractual obligations.
3. Add all current minimum debt payments.
4. Include irregular expenses and a realistic cash buffer.
5. Calculate remaining available cash.
6. Stress-test reduced income, delayed receipts, higher essential costs, and unexpected repairs.
7. Assess whether the new payment remains manageable in the downside scenario.

A low monthly payment can still be expensive if the term is long. A loan may be technically approved but unsuitable for the borrower's actual budget.

## Repayment strategies

### Highest-interest-first (debt avalanche)
Pay required minimums on all debts, then direct additional affordable payments to the debt with the highest effective cost. This generally minimizes interest when rates and terms are comparable.

### Smallest-balance-first (debt snowball)
Pay required minimums, then focus extra payments on the smallest balance. This may create visible progress and support motivation, but can cost more in interest.

### Hybrid prioritization
Address urgent arrears, legal or collateral risks, promotional-rate expiry, and high-cost balances first, then use a motivation-friendly sequence for the rest.

### Restructuring or negotiation
Where payments are no longer affordable, investigate legitimate hardship programs, revised terms, settlement options, or qualified local debt counseling. Compare the full revised cost and consequences before agreeing.

## BNPL and installment behavior

- Record every active installment in one consolidated calendar.
- Calculate the total monthly installment burden across providers.
- Include future installments in the budget before making a new purchase.
- Distinguish interest-free marketing from fees, late charges, price differences, and lost discounts.
- Avoid treating unused credit limits as income.
- Evaluate the purchase's necessity, useful life, and ability to pay without relying on future borrowing.
- Watch for installment stacking: individually small payments can collectively consume future income.

## New borrowing decision

Before borrowing, clarify:
- What specific problem or asset will the funds finance?
- Is borrowing necessary, or is there a lower-risk alternative?
- What is the complete cost?
- What income or cash source will repay it?
- What happens if that source is delayed, reduced, or lost?
- Is collateral or a personal guarantee at risk?
- Does the borrowing create a productive return or mainly accelerate consumption?
- Does it weaken emergency liquidity?

For productive borrowing, use Financial Leverage and Opportunity & Investment Evaluation for the separate investment/business case.

## Calculations

**Total monthly debt service** = sum of all required monthly debt and installment payments.

**Debt-service share of take-home income** = monthly debt service ÷ reliable monthly take-home income.

This is a diagnostic indicator, not a universal approval threshold. Interpret it with living costs, dependents, income volatility, and local lending context.

**Remaining monthly cash** = reliable take-home income − essential costs − required debt payments − committed obligations.

**Total repayment** = all scheduled payments + upfront fees + required add-ons + applicable settlement or balloon amounts.

**Interest saved by extra repayment** depends on rate, amortization method, fees, timing, and early-settlement rules; calculate from the actual contract or a verified schedule.

## AI workflow

1. Build a complete debt inventory before recommending a strategy.
2. Identify urgent arrears, legal notices, collateral exposure, and essential-service risks.
3. Verify rates, fees, terms, and local rules; mark missing contract details.
4. Model current cash flow and a downside scenario.
5. Preserve essential living costs and required minimum payments where feasible.
6. Compare avalanche, snowball, and hybrid strategies using both cost and behavioral fit.
7. Evaluate refinancing or restructuring by total cost, not only lower installments.
8. Provide a realistic payment sequence and review milestones.
9. If the user is in severe distress, prioritize contacting creditors and qualified local debt support rather than proposing speculative investments.

## Common failure modes

- Looking only at the installment amount.
- Forgetting multiple BNPL schedules.
- Extending debt terms without comparing total cost.
- Taking new debt to cover recurring budget deficits without addressing the cause.
- Using emergency savings to make optional extra payments and then borrowing again after a shock.
- Assuming all debts have the same legal consequences.
- Ignoring variable rates, fees, penalties, collateral, or guarantors.
