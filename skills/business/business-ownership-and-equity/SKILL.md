## Core concepts

### 1. Business model and expansion

A business may begin with one shop and expand through additional outlets, wholesale distribution, franchising, licensing, or company-owned branches.

These models are not interchangeable:
- **Company-owned branches:** The same company owns and operates additional locations.
- **Franchise:** An independent franchisee operates under a contractual license and pays agreed fees or royalties.
- **Wholesale/distribution:** The producer sells products to independent retailers or distributors.
- **Licensing:** Another party receives permission to use specified intellectual property or business assets under contract.

AI application:
- Identify which expansion model is actually being discussed.
- Explain who owns the outlet, brand, equipment, inventory, and operating company.
- Do not call every multi-outlet business a franchise.

### 2. Ownership versus management

Ownership refers to holding a legal or beneficial interest in a business. Management refers to responsibility for operating and making decisions within the authority granted.

A founder may own and manage a business, but ownership and management can be separated. A hired manager may operate a business without owning its shares.

AI application:
- Ask which legal entity owns the business assets and contracts.
- Distinguish founders, shareholders, directors, managers, franchisees, and employees.
- Avoid assuming that the person visible in a shop owns the whole company.

### 3. Equity and shares

Equity represents an ownership interest in a business after accounting for liabilities. In a company with share capital, shares represent units of ownership subject to the company's legal structure and share rights.

Illustrative example:
- A company has 1,000 ordinary shares.
- An investor owns 100 shares.
- The investor holds 10% of that share class, assuming all shares have equal rights and no other adjustments apply.

The percentage does not guarantee a fixed amount of profit, dividends, voting power, or sale proceeds. Those depend on share rights, company documents, financial results, law, and other conditions.

### 4. Capital, revenue, and profit

Do not confuse:
- **Revenue:** Money earned from sales before expenses.
- **Operating costs:** Costs of running the business.
- **Profit:** The amount remaining after relevant expenses under the applicable accounting basis.
- **Equity value:** The value attributable to owners after considering liabilities and valuation assumptions.
- **Cash flow:** Actual cash entering and leaving the business.

A growing shop network or factory does not automatically mean the owners are profitable or that the shares are valuable.

### 5. Share ownership and transfer

Shares may be issued by a company or transferred between holders, subject to applicable company law, constitutional documents, shareholder agreements, securities rules, and transaction terms.

AI application:
- Explain primary issuance versus secondary transfer.
- Clarify that buying an existing share from another holder does not necessarily send new capital to the company.
- Identify dilution when a company issues additional shares.
- Encourage review of financial statements, ownership records, rights, liabilities, and legal documents before an investment decision.

## Operational teaching framework

When a user asks how ownership or shares work:

1. **Start with a familiar business.** Use a shop, bakery, restaurant, or small manufacturer.
2. **Map the business structure.** Identify the operating entity, outlets, assets, staff, suppliers, and owners.
3. **Explain the expansion model.** Distinguish branches, franchise, licensing, and distribution.
4. **Separate roles.** Explain owner, shareholder, director, manager, franchisee, and employee.
5. **Introduce equity.** Use a clearly labeled numerical example and state its assumptions.
6. **Explain money flows.** Show sales, costs, profit, reinvestment, dividends, and capital contributions separately.
7. **Explain risks.** Include debt, dilution, illiquidity, business failure, governance, and legal restrictions.
8. **Check understanding.** Ask the user to explain who owns what and where money flows before moving to advanced topics.

## Example: a bakery grows

Assume a bakery starts with one shop. It may grow by:
- Opening another company-owned outlet.
- Selling products wholesale to other shops.
- Licensing its brand or franchising under a formal agreement.
- Creating a company that raises capital by issuing shares, if legally and commercially appropriate.

These choices have different implications for control, capital needs, operating responsibility, revenue, and risk. Do not assume issuing shares is always the best way to expand.

## AI guardrails

- Do not imply that a business expansion guarantees profit or increases share value.
- Do not invent ownership percentages, valuations, dividends, or financial results.
- Label all numerical examples as illustrative assumptions.
- Do not provide jurisdiction-specific legal conclusions without checking current local law.
- Distinguish education from personalized investment advice.
- Encourage due diligence and independent professional advice for material legal, tax, or investment decisions.
- Explain downside risk, including loss of invested capital.
