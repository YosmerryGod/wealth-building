# Financial Leverage & Other People's Money (OPM)

## Purpose

Help users compare ways to fund, acquire, access, and expand productive business assets. The skill covers owner capital, reinvestment, renting or leasing assets, customer-funded pre-orders, family or private funding, bank borrowing, and combinations of funding sources.

The goal is not to maximize borrowed money. It is to select a funding structure that fits the business model, customer demand, cash-flow timing, operational capacity, risk tolerance, and obligations to every party providing money or assets.

## Core concepts

### 1. Owner capital and reinvestment

Owner capital is money the owner contributes and places at risk in the business. Reinvestment means retaining sustainable business surplus to fund additional productive capacity instead of distributing or consuming all available cash.

Before reinvesting, separate:
- Revenue from collected cash.
- Gross profit from operating profit.
- Accounting profit from cash available.
- Cash needed for tax, suppliers, payroll, debt service, refunds, and other commitments.
- A reasonable reserve for disruptions.

Reinvest only cash that is genuinely available after required obligations and a suitable working-capital reserve.

### 2. Productive-asset ownership

Buying an asset gives the business ownership and potential control over its use, but requires capital and transfers asset risks to the owner.

Evaluate:
- Purchase and installation cost.
- Expected useful life and residual value.
- Capacity and expected utilization.
- Maintenance, storage, insurance, and replacement.
- Operating cash generated after all costs.
- Whether ownership creates a real advantage over renting or outsourcing.

An asset is not automatically productive merely because it can be used to generate sales. It must have real demand and positive economics.

### 3. Renting or leasing productive assets

Renting can provide access to an asset without paying its full purchase price upfront. It can reduce initial capital requirements, but the business may have recurring rental payments, usage restrictions, availability risk, and less control.

Compare ownership and rental using the same time horizon and realistic utilization assumptions:
- Upfront purchase price versus deposits and recurring rent.
- Maintenance and repair responsibilities.
- Contract duration, renewal, termination, and price changes.
- Downtime, replacement, and asset availability.
- Total cost over the expected use period.
- Flexibility if demand falls or the business changes.

Do not assume renting is always cheaper or that owning is always better. Choose based on total cost, risk transfer, flexibility, and cash-flow capacity.

### 4. Other People's Money (OPM)

OPM is a broad description of using money or resources provided by parties other than the business owner. It is not one legal or financial instrument. Identify the actual arrangement before recommending it.

Possible sources include:
- **Customer pre-orders or advance payments:** customers pay before delivery. This creates an obligation to provide the promised product or service, or handle refunds according to the agreed terms and applicable law.
- **Family or friends:** funds may be a loan, gift, equity contribution, or profit-sharing arrangement. Never assume which one. Clarify the amount, purpose, return or repayment, timeline, loss allocation, and what happens if the business fails.
- **Private investors or partners:** funds may be exchanged for ownership, profit share, governance rights, or another agreed return. Clarify control, dilution, reporting, exit, and loss allocation.
- **Bank or other formal credit:** borrowed money creates repayment obligations, interest or fees, covenants, collateral exposure, and possible default consequences.
- **Supplier credit:** goods or inputs are received now and paid for later. This creates a payable and depends on supplier terms and the business's ability to sell or use the inputs before payment is due.

Do not describe OPM as free capital. Every source has a cost, obligation, dependency, or risk—even when the cost is not an interest payment.

### 5. Customer-funded business and pre-orders

A pre-order or advance-payment model can help validate demand and fund production before delivery. It is appropriate only when the business can make a credible promise and fulfill it.

Before using customer money:
1. Confirm that the offer, quantity, price, specifications, and delivery date are clear.
2. Check supplier availability, production capacity, and realistic lead times.
3. Calculate the full cost of fulfilling every accepted order.
4. Separate customer advances from money that is free to spend.
5. Keep enough cash or a credible funding plan to fulfill orders or make required refunds.
6. Communicate delays, substitutions, cancellation, and refund terms transparently.
7. Do not use new customer advances to conceal an inability to fulfill earlier orders.

Pre-orders are not automatically proof of sustainable demand. Measure fulfilled orders, refunds, repeat purchases, and actual contribution margin.

### 6. Capital stacking

Capital stacking means combining multiple sources of funding for one business or project. A stack may include owner capital, retained earnings, customer advances, family loans, investor capital, supplier credit, or bank debt.

For every source, record:
- Provider and amount.
- Instrument type: equity, loan, advance payment, trade credit, lease, or other.
- Cost, interest, profit share, ownership dilution, fees, or non-cash obligations.
- Repayment or fulfillment date.
- Priority and consequences if cash is insufficient.
- Collateral, guarantees, covenants, and control rights.
- Whether the source is committed, conditional, or merely proposed.

Do not combine funding sources into one undifferentiated amount. A Rp300,000 family loan and a Rp300,000 customer pre-order may look identical as cash received, but they create different obligations and risks.

## Decision workflow: choose the funding model

When a user wants to start, fund, or expand a business:

1. **Define the business need.** Is funding needed for equipment, inventory, working capital, production capacity, marketing, or an asset used temporarily?
2. **Validate demand.** Identify paying customers, order frequency, willingness to pay, and evidence of repeat demand. Do not treat interest or views as confirmed sales.
3. **Determine the amount and timing.** Calculate how much cash is needed, when it is needed, and when the business realistically receives cash.
4. **Compare access models.** Consider buying, renting, leasing, outsourcing, or partnering before assuming an asset must be purchased.
5. **Compare funding sources.** Consider owner capital, reinvestment, pre-orders, family/friends, investors, supplier credit, and formal debt where appropriate.
6. **Classify each source correctly.** Identify whether it is equity, debt, advance payment, trade credit, lease, or another arrangement. Clarify ambiguous family or private funding before counting it.
7. **Calculate total cost and obligations.** Include interest, fees, profit share, dilution, rent, deposits, refunds, fulfillment, collateral, guarantees, and administrative costs.
8. **Match cash timing.** Check that customer receipts or operating cash arrive before supplier payments, payroll, rent, refunds, and debt installments fall due.
9. **Stress-test failure scenarios.** Model lower sales, price declines, higher costs, disease or asset loss, delayed delivery, customer cancellations, supplier disruption, and business interruption.
10. **Check execution capacity.** Confirm that staff, processes, permits, suppliers, quality control, storage, and distribution can support the funded expansion.
11. **Choose the least fragile feasible structure.** Prefer the smallest commitment that can validate the model and meet the goal without creating unmanageable obligations.
12. **Set stop and review conditions.** Define cash thresholds, sales targets, repayment triggers, and conditions under which the user should pause expansion or seek restructuring advice.

## Financial checks and useful calculations

Use the calculations that fit the funding structure. State assumptions and avoid false precision.

- **Net operating cash flow** = operating cash receipts − operating cash payments.
- **Contribution per unit** = selling price − variable cost per unit.
- **Break-even volume** = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit, where contribution is positive.
- **Total borrowing cost** = interest + fees + other financing costs, in addition to principal repayment.
- **Debt-service coverage ratio (DSCR)** = cash flow available for debt service ÷ scheduled principal and interest payments. Define the numerator consistently and use conservative assumptions.
- **Rental total cost** = deposits/fees that are not refundable + recurring rent + related operating costs over the comparison period.
- **Pre-order fulfillment funding gap** = cash required to fulfill accepted orders and meet refunds/commitments − cash safely available for those obligations.
- **Cash runway** = available unrestricted cash ÷ average periodic net cash burn, when burn is reasonably stable.

For loans, distinguish the principal received from interest and fees. For equity, distinguish capital received from revenue and explain ownership dilution. For pre-orders, distinguish customer advances from earned sales and available free cash.

## Funding structure comparison

| Source or model | Main benefit | Main obligation or risk |
|---|---|---|
| Owner capital | No external repayment schedule or dilution | Owner bears the capital loss and opportunity cost |
| Reinvestment | Uses internally generated funds | Can weaken reserves if too much cash is reinvested |
| Buy asset | Ownership and control | High upfront cost, maintenance, idle-capacity risk |
| Rent/lease asset | Lower initial purchase requirement, flexibility | Recurring payments, contract terms, access and availability risk |
| Customer pre-order | Can validate demand and bring cash before delivery | Must fulfill orders or handle refunds; reputation and legal exposure |
| Family/friend loan | May offer flexible terms | Relationship damage and unclear repayment expectations |
| Investor/equity | No fixed loan installment in a conventional equity structure | Ownership dilution, governance rights, shared upside and control |
| Supplier credit | Inputs available before cash payment | Payables fall due; supplier dependence and late-payment risk |
| Bank loan | Can fund a defined expansion | Principal, interest, fees, collateral, covenants, and default risk |

## Risk management and safeguards

- Do not recommend borrowing merely because a lender is willing to provide money.
- Do not call customer advances, refundable deposits, or loan proceeds profit.
- Do not promise investors, family members, or lenders a return that the business cannot support.
- Do not treat a promise to repay twice the amount as a harmless family arrangement. Clarify whether it is a loan, investment, profit share, or another contract; assess affordability and legal implications.
- Do not use customer pre-orders as a substitute for a viable fulfillment plan.
- Do not use one new funding source to conceal unpaid obligations to an earlier provider.
- Do not stack multiple debts without modeling the combined repayment schedule and priority of claims.
- Do not pledge essential personal or family assets without understanding the downside and independent advice where appropriate.
- Keep written records of amounts, terms, dates, rights, obligations, and what happens if the business underperforms.
- Separate business and personal money where practical.
- Maintain an appropriate cash reserve; do not deploy all available cash into expansion.
- Consider local consumer, lending, tax, contract, securities, and business rules. Refer complex legal, tax, or regulated financing questions to qualified local professionals.
- If conservative cash flow cannot cover essential operations and contractual obligations, recommend reducing scope, delaying expansion, renegotiating terms, or not taking the funding.

## Practical lesson

The transferable lesson is not "use other people's money at any cost." It is:

**Choose the right asset-access model, validate real demand, understand exactly whose money is being used, price every obligation, and expand only when conservative cash flow and operational capacity can support the commitments.**
