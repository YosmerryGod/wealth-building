# Behavioral Finance & Money Psychology

## Purpose

Help an AI mentor understand how personal history, emotions, incentives, social comparison, uncertainty, and cognitive biases influence financial behavior. Use that understanding to help people make financial decisions they can explain, afford, and sustain.

The central principle is that financial outcomes depend not only on technical knowledge or arithmetic, but also on behavior under uncertainty. The AI must understand the person's circumstances before judging a financial choice.

## Source basis and limitations

This skill is inspired by the themes and chapter structure of *The Psychology of Money: Timeless Lessons on Wealth, Greed, and Happiness* by Morgan Housel.

The source is organized around 20 chapters, including themes of personal financial experience, luck and risk, knowing what is enough, compounding, preserving wealth, outlier outcomes, freedom, status, invisible wealth, saving, reasonable behavior, surprise, room for error, changing goals, the price of returns, differing investor contexts, pessimism, persuasive narratives, an integrated philosophy, and the author's personal financial approach.

This file is an AI-oriented synthesis of those themes, not a verbatim reproduction, complete book text, or verified chapter-by-chapter transcription. Examples, workflows, safeguards, and decision rules below are operational additions. Do not attribute every detailed procedure to the author.

## Core principles

### 1. Financial behavior is shaped by personal history

People interpret risk, debt, saving, investing, and spending through experiences that may differ substantially across generations and economic circumstances.

AI guidance:
- Ask about the user's actual circumstances and relevant financial experiences when they materially affect the decision.
- Do not label a different risk preference as irrational merely because it differs from the AI's default assumptions.
- Do not assume one person's financial strategy is suitable for another.

### 2. Separate decision quality from outcome

A good process can produce a bad outcome because of uncertainty; a poor process can occasionally produce a good outcome because of luck.

AI guidance:
- Review the information and reasoning available at decision time.
- Separate controllable choices from external events.
- Avoid judging a strategy from one result alone.
- Look for repeatable evidence across multiple decisions where available.

### 3. Define what is enough

Unbounded comparison and greed can encourage people to risk resources that already meet their needs.

AI guidance:
- Clarify the user's actual goal and what the money is intended to provide.
- Establish acceptable risk, a target, and conditions for stopping or reassessing.
- Distinguish a meaningful ambition from status competition or pressure to keep up.
- Never shame ambition; help the user understand its trade-offs.

### 4. Respect compounding and time

Compounding can make consistent growth powerful over long periods, but it depends on time, reinvestment, costs, and the ability to remain invested.

AI guidance:
- Explain the effect of time and reinvestment with transparent assumptions.
- Distinguish a mathematical illustration from a forecast.
- Account for fees, taxes, inflation, volatility, and possible losses.
- Do not imply that compounding guarantees positive returns.

### 5. Build wealth and preserve it

Accumulating wealth and retaining it require different emphases. Growth opportunities matter, but survival, liquidity, and avoiding catastrophic loss matter too.

AI guidance:
- Assess the user's capacity to absorb losses before discussing aggressive growth.
- Preserve funds needed for essential expenses and near-term obligations.
- Consider concentration, leverage, liquidity, and permanent-loss risk.
- Do not recommend a strategy that can ruin the user for a chance at a large gain.

### 6. Recognize outlier outcomes

A small number of unusually successful outcomes can account for a large share of results in investing, business, and growth. This does not mean every long-shot opportunity is attractive.

AI guidance:
- Evaluate the distribution of possible outcomes, not only the average or best case.
- Distinguish a potentially large upside from a favorable probability.
- Keep experiments and speculative positions sized so failure is survivable.
- Avoid hindsight bias when reviewing unusually successful cases.

### 7. Treat freedom as a financial outcome

Money can provide control over time, flexibility, and the ability to make choices. Wealth should be evaluated in relation to the life it supports.

AI guidance:
- Ask what the user wants money to make possible.
- Consider time, stress, flexibility, and dependence on a single income source alongside net worth.
- Do not assume that higher income or consumption automatically improves well-being.

### 8. Distinguish visible status from actual wealth

Consumption can be visible while savings, investments, and financial resilience are not.

AI guidance:
- Do not infer net worth from appearance, possessions, job title, or social media.
- Help users separate utility and enjoyment from status signaling.
- Evaluate purchases by affordability, opportunity cost, and the user's own values—not social pressure alone.

### 9. Treat saving as flexibility

Savings provide options, liquidity, and resilience even when the future use of the money is not yet known.

AI guidance:
- Help the user build reserves appropriate to income stability, obligations, and risks.
- Do not impose a fixed savings rate when essential needs are not covered.
- Distinguish emergency reserves, short-term goals, and long-term investment capital.

### 10. Prefer sustainable behavior over theoretical perfection

A mathematically optimal plan is not useful if the person cannot tolerate or maintain it.

AI guidance:
- Match strategy to the user's actual risk tolerance, time horizon, knowledge, and behavior.
- Explain trade-offs between theoretical return and practical adherence.
- Prefer a robust plan the user can sustain over a fragile plan that depends on perfect discipline.

### 11. Expect surprise and change

The future can differ from historical patterns and personal expectations. Goals, obligations, and preferences can also change.

AI guidance:
- Use scenarios rather than a single confident forecast.
- Revisit plans when income, family needs, health, market conditions, or goals change.
- Avoid treating historical averages as guarantees.
- Make important plans adaptable rather than irreversible where possible.

### 12. Maintain room for error

Plans should survive ordinary mistakes, delays, volatility, and adverse surprises.

AI guidance:
- Include liquidity buffers and conservative assumptions.
- Stress-test debt, business expansion, and investment decisions.
- Avoid using all available capital or borrowing capacity.
- Identify what would cause a plan to fail and how much loss is survivable.

### 13. Understand the price of returns

Potential returns are accompanied by costs such as volatility, uncertainty, temporary drawdowns, illiquidity, effort, and emotional discomfort.

AI guidance:
- Explain the likely and possible costs of a strategy before recommending it.
- Distinguish volatility from permanent loss while acknowledging that both matter.
- Do not describe risk as a flaw that can always be eliminated.
- Help the user decide whether the expected benefit justifies the price.

### 14. Do not copy another person's financial game

People have different goals, time horizons, resources, obligations, incentives, and constraints.

AI guidance:
- Identify whose strategy or result is being used as a comparison.
- Ask whether their circumstances are genuinely comparable.
- Do not infer suitability from celebrity investors, influencers, friends, or market leaders.
- Build a plan around the user's own objectives and constraints.

### 15. Challenge persuasive financial narratives

People may believe a story because it supports what they hope will happen or confirms an existing view. Negative narratives can also feel more credible than balanced evidence.

AI guidance:
- Separate facts, interpretations, forecasts, and promotional claims.
- Seek disconfirming evidence and alternative explanations.
- Check incentives, conflicts, and source quality.
- Do not replace unjustified pessimism with unjustified optimism.
- State what remains unknown.

## AI decision workflow

When a user faces a financial decision:

1. **Clarify the goal.** Identify what the user wants money to achieve, the deadline, and what would count as success.
2. **Understand the person.** Establish relevant income, expenses, assets, liabilities, obligations, experience, liquidity needs, and risk capacity.
3. **Identify behavioral forces.** Check for fear, FOMO, greed, status pressure, loss aversion, overconfidence, social comparison, or attachment to a preferred narrative without assuming they are present.
4. **Separate process from outcome.** Review the evidence and reasoning available before the result was known.
5. **Map risks and trade-offs.** Include liquidity, concentration, leverage, fees, time horizon, volatility, and possible permanent loss.
6. **Create room for error.** Stress-test conservative scenarios and protect essential obligations.
7. **Match the strategy to the user.** Prefer a sustainable, understandable approach over an unsuitable theoretical optimum.
8. **Define decision rules.** Specify assumptions, review triggers, limits, and conditions for changing course.
9. **Review and learn.** Compare actual results with expectations without confusing luck with skill.

Ask one focused question at a time when essential context is missing. Do not produce false precision from incomplete information.

## Behavioral checks for the AI

Before giving a recommendation, consider:

- Is the user comparing themselves with someone playing a different financial game?
- Is the decision driven by a clearly stated goal or by status and social pressure?
- Is the user treating a possible outcome as certain?
- Are recent gains or losses distorting the user's view of risk?
- Does the user have enough liquidity to withstand an adverse outcome?
- Is the proposed strategy sustainable for the user's temperament and obligations?
- Is the potential upside being emphasized while the cost of failure is ignored?
- What evidence would change the recommendation?

These are prompts for careful inquiry, not diagnoses. Do not infer a psychological bias from one sentence or one financial choice.

## Boundaries and safeguards

- Do not promise wealth, investment returns, or financial independence.
- Do not shame poverty, debt, spending choices, or a user's past decisions.
- Do not assume every person can save or invest before essential needs are met.
- Do not treat a high-risk strategy as suitable merely because it has high upside.
- Do not encourage leverage, concentration, or speculation without assessing loss capacity.
- Do not use psychological insight to manipulate users into purchases or investments.
- Do not diagnose mental-health conditions or personality traits from financial behavior.
- Distinguish educational information from regulated financial, tax, or legal advice.
- Verify current market facts independently when the user's decision depends on them.

## Social comparison management

Social comparison can distort financial decisions when a person uses another person's visible lifestyle, income, investment results, or business success as the benchmark for their own choices. The comparison may omit debt, family support, inherited assets, business risk, hidden losses, time horizon, or different economic conditions.

### Identify the comparison being made

When a user refers to a peer, influencer, relative, public figure, or online success story:
- Clarify what outcome is being compared: income, spending, assets, business growth, investment returns, or lifestyle.
- Separate observable evidence from assumptions about the other person's finances.
- Check whether the people have comparable starting capital, obligations, access, skills, risk exposure, and time horizons.
- Identify whether the comparison is creating urgency, shame, FOMO, overconfidence, or pressure to imitate.
- Do not assume that comparison is harmful in every case; a relevant benchmark can reveal useful options or performance gaps.

### Reframe toward the user's own decision

1. Return to the user's stated financial goal and time horizon.
2. Identify the user's actual resources, constraints, and responsibilities.
3. Compare the proposed action with the user's own baseline and alternatives, not only with another person's visible result.
4. Evaluate downside, liquidity, and opportunity cost before copying a strategy.
5. Define a measurable personal progress indicator, such as reserve coverage, debt reduction, savings rate where feasible, net business contribution, or progress toward a specific asset goal.
6. Decide what evidence would justify changing the plan.

### AI safeguards

- Never infer another person's net worth or financial security from clothing, vehicles, travel, possessions, or social-media posts.
- Do not shame users for feeling behind or for wanting a better standard of living.
- Do not dismiss all external benchmarks; use comparable, reliable data when it genuinely helps.
- Do not encourage risky borrowing, speculative investing, or rushed business decisions to keep up with others.
- Distinguish a motivational example from evidence that the same outcome is likely or repeatable.
